Plate 01

Three planes read one host

the same counters, three readers, three schedules

Where the planes come from

  • Plane 1 is vCenter's own history, shaped by its statistics level; the decision beneath this plate sets that level.
  • Plane 2 is the platform's input, read through vStats by each vCenter adapter; what that means for vCenter's level is the same decision.
  • Plane 3 is new in 9.1 and optional. It is a pull of its own with its own store, addressed per VCF instance, and it is the only plane that serves 2-second values doc + live.

What a source serves

  • Real-Time Metrics' catalog holds 437 names, and each source collects a subset of them (9.1.1, unchanged from 9.1) live.
  • Five acquisition profiles, all Essentials: ESXi 20 s, vCenter 20 s, vCenter 60 s, a PerformanceManager set served at 20 s although its profile name carries 300, and ESX Top 2 s, every name of which the ESXi 20 s profile also serves on 9.1.1 live.
  • A source's series count is an inventory measurement, not a platform constant: it grows with the hosts, VMs and devices behind the source. Read yours with count() per source before you size anything on it; plate 15 takes it live.

Tradeoff

For the vSphere estate the collection problem is already solved by plane 2. A second 20-second pull from vCenter, whether by a warehouse or by a monitoring platform's vCenter integration, adds load on vpxd and truth to nothing, because the analytics store already keeps the 5-minute mean and the peak keys. The strategy question is therefore extraction, not collection: which plane, at which grain, for which decision.

Decision: vCenter's statistics level

  • Level 1 suits long term monitoring and levels 3 and 4 short term troubleshooting; raising 3 or 4 can grow vCenter's vpxd process when it cannot save statistics as fast as it collects them, so watch its size after any raise. Read a counter's level from your vCenter's counter catalog, not from a summary list. doc “When the statistics levels, level 3 or level 4 are used beyond the default value, it may cause one particular process, vpxd, to sustain memory growth, if it cannot save the statistics information to the database as quickly as required.” Statistics Levels (9.1)
  • VCF Operations reads every counter it collects through vStats at 20 seconds, whatever vCenter's statistics level, so vCenter can stay at level 1 for Operations; a higher level only makes vCenter itself keep more, as your own fallback. live

Observed on the product

  • vCenter 9.1.1's counter catalog places cpu.usage and cpu.ready at level 1, cpu.demand, cpu.costop, cpu.idle, mem.active, mem.swapped and the network packet and drop counters at level 2, and cpu.readiness and sys.osUptime at level 4, lower than Broadcom's 9.1 Statistics Levels page lists most of them. A counter above an interval's statistics level is not kept for that interval, so read your intervals' levels. live (observed 2026-11-03)
Plate 02

What each store keeps and loses

the 5-minute value is a mean; the peak survives only in the peak keys or on the real-time plane

What survives, and where

  • The stored 5-minute value is the mean of the fifteen 20-second samples in the cycle ending at its timestamp; against a separately collected 20-second stream it differs by a few percent, the collectors sampling at different moments rather than aggregating differently (one VM and one host over three hours, read on 9.1) live.
  • Twelve VM keys keep the within-cycle maximum, the 20_sec_peak kind (CPU ready, co-stop, overlap, other wait; memory contention; network usage and packets per second; virtual-disk latency; the guest's run queue, disk queue, context switches, page-out rate); none of the peak-named keys a host collects is of this kind (read on 9.1), and clusters were not read. Twelve is the most a machine carried on 9.1.1, of the 19 in the VM catalog, and not a guarantee: a machine without guest-level collection carries eight, the four guest keys absent, and some carry a count in between. Build a panel on the twelve and it renders empty on the machines that have fewer, so count them per machine before you promise the view. The keys named "highest of all instances" are the busiest disk, vCPU, core, or adapter averaged over the cycle, not a time peak. The one testable within-cycle key tracks the window maximum, and plate 11 shows the 20-second plane reproducing it doc + live.
  • Server-side hourly AVG and MAX are exact against the native points, so downsampling on the server costs no truth live.

What this settles for the raw-versus-real-time argument

  • Utilization, rightsizing, and capacity read means and long windows; the 5-minute grain serves them without loss that matters.
  • The mean hides contention bursts badly; they belong to the peak keys or to the real-time plane, for a scoped set, in region.
  • Live troubleshooting is not a BI decision; it rides the real-time plane and stays there.

Tradeoff

Pulling native 5-minute points for everything costs about twelve times the hourly tier for no decision gain, and it still does not recover peaks, because the peak was averaged away before it was stored. Spend native resolution only on the contention keys of a scoped hot-set, and take the peak keys where they exist.

Plate 03

Horizons and extraction deadlines

how long each grain exists anywhere, on a log scale

The settings behind the bars, at the defaults an instance reports

  • TIME_SERIES_DATA_RETENTION_IN_MONTHS = 6, then ROLLUP_TIME_SERIES_DATA_RETENTION_IN_MONTHS = 36 live.
  • REALTIME_DATA_RETENTION_IN_DAYS = 15; ESX_TOP_DATA_RETENTION_IN_HOURS = 24 live. The 6-hour figure in the 9.1 pages is the ESX Top view's maximum time range, not retention doc.
  • The vCenter rollup ladder is documented; the real-time window is short and is not a store doc.
  • The platform reports each setting's default beside its value. /api/deployment/config/globalsettings answers a key and its value, and /api/deployment/config/globalsettings/metadata answers each key's default, unit and range, so an instance can be asked whether a retention setting differs from its default; the harness's collection_planes.py prints both and flags a tuned key. It cannot be asked when a setting changed or what it was before, which matters most when you inherit one, so record what you set live.

What lazy enforcement means for a pipeline

  • A served horizon can run past its setting for days and then drop in one step. Never plan on data older than the setting being there, and never assume a fixed cut.
  • Watermark on bucket-end timestamps and reconcile absence against inventory and collector health before calling a gap a decision.

Tradeoff

Longer retention on the platform buys extraction slack, not analysis: the warehouse still needs its own tiers. The cheaper move is to extract each grain once, well inside its horizon, and to keep the platform's retention at defaults unless a documented limit or a proven gap says otherwise.

Plate 04

Decisions and the grain they consume

granularity is a per-decision parameter, not a property of the pipeline

The platform already computes the decisions

  • Rightsizing is the maximum projected utilization out to 30 days past the policy's Time Remaining warning threshold, shaped by the policy risk level doc + live.
  • Sharing and reallocation have four public surfaces: reclaim per reason, rightsize per reason, workload placement, and the recommendation catalog, a dimension table of definitions, plus 18 per-VM decision statkeys live.
  • Capacity ratios live in the policy (CPU, memory and disk overcommit; read yours from your policy's capacity settings); the risk level is a quantile dial on recency-weighted demand, so a warehouse percentile over a fixed window is a different estimator and will disagree by design.

Path chooser

  • Hypervisor-visible signal? Operations, extracted. Never a second pull.
  • A burst or contention question? Peak keys first; a scoped 20-second hot-set from Real-Time Metrics if the key does not exist.
  • Inside the guest? An in-guest agent. Off vSphere? Zabbix. Identity and business context? The CMDB.

Tradeoff

Recomputing a decision in the warehouse from raw counters buys independence at the price of disagreement with the platform's own number. Read the platform's decision, record the policy's risk level beside it as provenance, and compute only what the platform does not: cross-estate comparisons, cost, and history beyond its retention.

Plate 05

The catalog: what a warehouse asks for, and where it lives

nine families, the vCenter counter with its statistics level, the Operations key, the tier; every key checked against a live statkey list

What the levels settle

  • vCenter keeps a counter beyond its real-time window only at or above the counter's level, and the default is 1. Demand, co-stop, active and swapped memory, and the packet and drop counters sit at level 2; swap wait at 3; readiness and OS uptime at 4 live.
  • Operations reads every counter here through vStats at 20 seconds whatever the level says, so a list that would force the level up on vCenter is already served at level 1 by plane 2 live.
  • The peak keys are two kinds. Twelve 20_sec_peak keys on a VM keep the highest 20-second sample of the cycle; the peak_ keys, "Highest ... of all instances" in the definitions in VMware guidance, are the busiest disk, vCPU, core, or adapter averaged over the cycle, and a host read on 9.1 collected only those doc + live.

What only Operations or Tools produces

  • The guest family through VMware Tools: free, needed, and usable memory, swap space remaining, page rates, run and disk queues, context switches, filesystem totals. Conditional on Tools; a blank is absence, never zero live.
  • The decision keys: oversized and undersized with the reclaimable amounts, the recommended size under the policy's risk level, idle, snapshot space and count. Read them; never recompute them live.
  • Instanced families (virtual-disk latency, IOPS, outstanding IO; network packets and drops) carry the VM value under the Aggregate of all instances instance; the plain key returns nothing live.

Watch point

A catalog lists more keys than a resource collects: on 9.1.1 some of the peak keys in the adapter's VirtualMachine catalog are collected on no machine at all; the handbook chapter "Operations content is code" counts the catalog against what returns a value. Read a resource's own statkey list once and bind those names; a view, a super metric, or an extract bound to a catalog name renders a dash and teaches nothing.

The outage lesson

A stop in collection, an upgrade with snapshot reverts for instance, leaves no stored metrics for any vCenter adapter for as long as it lasts. An extract without a reconciliation step lands zero demand for every object across it. A fleet-wide plateau in the age of the newest point is the signature of a collection outage, not of a quiet estate.

Plate 07

Decide in region, ship the thin layer

one Operations instance per region, the real-time plane per VCF instance, one collector per domain beyond vSphere, the warehouse in the middle

Limits in VMware guidance that bound the shape doc

  • One Operations instance per fleet; one fleet per region is a named scenario; the cloud proxy is mandatory in the first 9.1 instance and outbound only.
  • Unified proxy sizes: Small up to 16,000 VMs, Standard 16,000 to 80,000. Node limits from the 9.0 table: Medium 30,000 objects and 5 million metrics; Large 44,000 and 8 million; Extra Large 100,000 and 20 million.
  • Proxy to node: RTT under 50 ms, 15 to 200 Mbps. Persistence buffers at most one hour, less when the buffer runs short of space; a collector group fails over after 10 minutes.

Why shape B

  • A single global instance puts every region's collection behind one WAN and one node ceiling; a per-region instance keeps collection local and ships what is small.
  • The WAN carries decisions and hourly aggregates, which compress a 10,000-VM region to about 288 MB a day for hourly AVG and MAX; native points would be six times that, and the real-time plane, which never crosses, more again.
  • The same shape holds beyond vSphere: one collector per domain per region keeps the raw series local, computes the hourly statistics at the edge, and ships them as their own lane; not every domain has to pass through Operations to reach the warehouse (plate 08).

Tradeoff

Regional instances cost a second set of policies, groups, and dashboards to keep consistent, which is a content-management problem with a known cure (versioned, generated content). A global instance costs WAN bandwidth, a hard node ceiling, and an hour of buffer as the only protection against a link outage. The second cost is structural; the first is procedural.

Choosing the collector

  • One collector per domain, one agent per host. Two agents for one signal is the failure mode; an agent posting to Operations and a second agent do not share a guest.
  • Choose by where the data must live. When the data must exist in Operations, pick the door by source type: open-source Telegraf for guest counters, viewed there and shared with the application team; the builder for any API or Prometheus source; a stable pack where one exists; the API push for derived numbers. A neutral collector's own store when the data must not depend on the VCF stack, for residency, another team's ownership, or an estate that is mostly not vSphere.
  • Network hardware runs no agent. Poll it, subscribe to it, or receive its flows, from the domain collector or from a network-monitoring platform such as Broadcom's DX NetOps or AppNeta. VCF Operations for Networks reads the flows the VMware estate itself exports, vSphere Distributed Switch and NSX IPFIX, and is not the physical network's monitor. Inside Operations, the Network Devices pack follows DX NetOps as the operations lens on devices and the SNMP ingestion path.

What every domain ships

  • Hourly min, avg, max, count per series, computed where the collector already knows how: BasicStats, recording rules, or trends. The raw series stay in the domain's regional store, as native points stay in Operations.
  • A daily gold layer where the domain computes decisions of its own; otherwise the hourly tier is the whole feed.
  • Its own key: the vCenter pair for vSphere, a CI for a server or a device; cross-domain joins follow the CMDB's relationships.

Tradeoff

A neutral collector buys independence from the platform and one pipeline for every operating system and device, at the price of a second store to run and a second identity to keep straight. A door into Operations buys the one extraction contract for that domain, native rollups and the same key discipline, at the price of the node's object and metric limits and a dependency on the VCF stack. Both are sound; the builder's Prometheus source lets one collector serve both, and two agents on one host is what is not.

Plate 09

One key across every system

a managed object ID is unique only inside its vCenter; the pair is unique everywhere and survives nothing that moves

Verified equalities live

  • Operations VMEntityObjectID equals the vCenter MOID; VMEntityVCID equals vCenter about.instanceUuid; VMEntityInstanceUUID equals the VM's config.instanceUuid.
  • Real-Time Metrics labels carry the MOIDs and the vCenter's name or IP, never its UUID; the UUID is the sourceId you pass, so stamp it.

CMDB side doc

  • The vCenter model keys a VM as a cmdb_ci_vmware_instance plus a guest server CI; the identifier fields are the vCenter UUID and the MOID.
  • The Operations management pack maps object_id from VMEntityObjectID and vcenter_uuid from VMEntityVCID, the same pair.

Watch point

Keying on the MOID alone works until the second vCenter, keying on the name works until the first rename, and keying on the pair works until the machine moves. All three fail silently: rows join to the wrong object, or to no object, and every decision downstream inherits it. On 9.1 a relocated VM leaves an Operations resource on each side of the move, both carrying its instance UUID: its history on the old one and only what follows the move on the new.

Plate 10

The seven rules for reading Real-Time Metrics

seven behaviors of the PromQL API that an extractor must encode, all measured

The extractor's seven lines. Pin the profile with a label matcher. Read cardinality with count by (profile) and partition by host so no call nears 101. Measure cadence as value-change spacing at a fine step. Stamp rows with sourceId and profile. Treat the truncation warning and an empty answer as errors to investigate, never as zeros. Write labels lowercase and take the names from the metadata endpoint, never from a picker. Never rate a gauge.

Plate 11

Two planes, one set of samples: the proof, and the calls

the store's mean and peak keys are rollups of the 20-second series; the seven calls a pipeline makes; ten queries built for the strategy, verified

What the comparison settles

  • The guest family matches: median mean and peak ratios of 1.00 over 36 buckets on each VM tested, the mean within half a percent (9.1.1, 2026-10-19, as on 9.1). The two planes are reading the same 20-second samples live.
  • Network usage sits at a median of 0.97 for the mean and 0.97 to 1.01 for the peak (9.1.1, 2026-10-19): two collection paths sampling at different phases, not two truths live.
  • Two guest keys are normalized in the store and raw on the real-time plane: the run queue per vCPU, the disk queue divided by 100. A join across planes scales per key first live.

What it settles for the design

  • A warehouse that extracts the store's mean and peak keys and the hourly MAX rollup keeps what a 20-second feed would give it for these families, without the volume.
  • The real-time plane earns its lane for what the store has no key for: the 2-second tail, per-core hot spots, the contention series of a scoped set within hours.
  • The row key is the same on both planes: sourceId and the object label are the store's (VMEntityVCID, VMEntityObjectID) pair, so the hot-set joins the tiers with no lookup.

What not to do, one line each. No rate() on a gauge: the engine reads a dip as a counter reset. No offset: it parses and does nothing. No subquery in a range call. No unpinned name that serves two cadences. No join across planes without the per-key scale check. No key copied from a picker without the metadata endpoint's word that the source serves it.

Plate 13

Three shapes, one recommendation

what crosses the WAN decides the topology, and residency follows from where the instance runs

The pattern to read in yours

  • A single analytics node, and every site adapter pinned to that site's own unified proxy, the node's own collector holding only the platform's own adapters, none of them vCenter, NSX or vSAN: the documented per-site pattern, which topology.py reads on your instance doc + live.
  • Persistence on with a collector group of one means high availability is off and each proxy is its site's single point of collection: a choice, which topology.py prints as a note live.
  • VMware guidance offers two upgrades on top of it: a collector group for high availability, accepting the 10-minute failover, and a second proxy where one is near its size limit doc.

Residency follows from the mechanics

  • The proxy connects outbound only and buffers for at most an hour; the single Operations instance of a fleet holds the time series doc.
  • So where the instance runs is where the raw telemetry lives. A region-local instance is the residency mechanism, and only decisions and rollups cross the border. Shape A cannot offer that; shape B does by construction. A region-local domain collector does the same for the agent and network domains.
  • VMware guidance read for this atlas carries no Operations-specific residency statement; this is derived from the documented mechanics, and is labelled as such.

Tradeoff

Inside each region, use the documented pattern: one proxy per site, persistence on, a collector group where a site has more than one proxy, one group per VCF instance. The extractor reads the regional node and ships decisions and rollups to the center. A regional instance costs a second set of policies and dashboards to keep consistent, a content problem with a known cure; a global instance costs the WAN, the node ceiling and an hour of buffer as the only outage protection, which no procedure removes.

Watch point

Check residency against what the thin tiers actually carry. Object names, host addresses and CMDB attributes travel with the rollups and the decision layer, and may be in scope for a data-transfer review even when the counters are not.

Plate 14

Outages become a data-quality rule

every layer of the topology fails with its own signature in the data, and absence is never a value

What an outage leaves

  • Through a proxy outage of days, a view's 7-day p95 columns keep reading while its current-value columns go blank: windows tolerate what current values do not live.
  • A stop in collection, an upgrade with snapshot reverts for instance, leaves no stored metrics for any vCenter adapter while it lasts; an extract without reconciliation lands zero demand for every object live.

The rule in the pipeline

  • A resource missing from a response had no data in the window. Count what was asked against what came back, and land the difference as absence.
  • Compare against inventory and collector health before a gap becomes a decision, and keep the real-time store's horizon in the lineage of any hot-set figure.

Reconcile absence before landing it. A fleet-wide plateau in the age of the newest point is the signature of a collection outage, not of a quiet estate.

Plate 15

Size each tier from your own coefficients

raw volume is objects times keys times cycles times regions; the calculator below holds the arithmetic and you supply the coefficients

What the example inputs show

  • Shape B moves roughly a thirtieth of what shape A moves, and the 5-minute data stays where it was collected; the decisions read the same either way.
  • The real-time store is the largest volume on the page and never crosses the WAN: even a small source keeps millions of samples a day in its region.

What this sheet cannot know about your estate

  • The 9.1 configuration maximums and fleet latency figures: the 9.1 pages route to single-page sites that did not render for this atlas, so the 9.0 table is the latest numeric one used, and marked.
  • Your own coefficients until you type them into the table, your collector groups and persistence flags, and your WAN round trip from each proxy to its node.

Watch point

The firehose is a choice, not a requirement. Shape A moves the platform's full 5-minute collection across the WAN so one node can hold it; shape B moves hourly rollups and a daily decision layer. Size from your own coefficients before either is signed off.

Plate 16

The gold layer, by decision

each of the four decisions already exists as a derived output with a public surface; the extract reads it rather than recomputing it

What reconciles, and what does not

  • The object view and the datacenter list agree: one VM read 2 vCPU and 6 GB oversized on its own statkeys and the same on the rightsize list. Units differ, KB and MHz on statkeys, vCPU and GB on the lists, so normalize once at load live.
  • The internal optimization endpoints return the same per-VM numbers and need the unsupported-API header. Read the public paged endpoints and watch their total count; keep the internal ones for cross-checks live.
  • Capacity runway rides the allocation model while rightsizing rides demand: confirm the policy model per cluster before trusting either.

The hypervisor sizes, the guest confirms

  • The hypervisor view (active memory, demand, contention) is what a rightsize or a reclaim changes, because it is what the scheduler hands the machine. It is the driving signal.
  • The guest view (in-OS CPU, filesystem occupancy) confirms that an opportunity the hypervisor found is safe to act on. It is the gate, not the sensor: an agent deployed to drive rightsizing measures the wrong layer.

Read the decision; record its risk level beside it. The policy's risk level is a quantile dial on a recency-weighted demand history, so the platform's recommendation is already a percentile-style projection. A warehouse percentile over a fixed window is a different estimator that will disagree by design.

Tradeoff

The surface a pipeline reads through changes over a product's life: an endpoint is added, a key is renamed, a plane appears. Make the extractor a source-abstracted adapter, one connector per surface behind one interface, so a change to the collection plane swaps a connector rather than the pipeline.

Plate 17

The CMDB join: the same pair on the other side

ServiceNow keys a VMware object on the managed object reference under the vCenter UUID; one VM is two CIs, and a move treats them differently

What the join reads from the CMDB

  • Owner, site and lifecycle are base CI fields: owned_by, managed_by, support_group, location, company, department, operational_status, and the CSDM life cycle stage and status. Read them as slowly changing dimensions; telemetry does not carry them doc.
  • The pack maps object_id from VMEntityObjectID and vcenter_uuid from VMEntityVCID: the same pair as plate 09, so no mapping table is needed doc.

What it settles

  • Choose UUID and POPULATE_ONLY before the CMDB is populated; changing the identifier source afterwards orphans every CI written under the other format.
  • Let the daily inventory diff drive retirement. Deleted objects linger 168 hours in Operations at the default, and a decommissioned VM still emitting telemetry is a data-quality signal, not a decision input.

Watch point

A relocation that the CMDB records as a new instance CI under a surviving guest CI is the CMDB reporting a move. A pipeline that counts instance CIs reads it as a new machine. Join through the guest CI, or through the continuity ledger of plate 09, before counting anything.

Plate 18

Where each signal waits on its way in, and for how long

every hop between a source and its store keeps a signal on its own clock; set your outage against each one

Read your own outage

  • Pick the hop that fails and for how long. A source or hold row whose bar ends left of the dark line runs out before the outage does, and what it held past that is gone at that hop.
  • A hop that disk ends has no time until you give it your volume an hour; the plate divides the record's disk by it, and the answer is arithmetic, not a documented figure.
  • Takeover and readable-after rows are waits, not stores: a takeover shorter than the outage is what a collector group loses, and readable-after is how long to wait before you call a source silent.

The rule in the design

  • Set persistence, a second proxy and vCenter's Past Day level from the outage you plan for, not from the defaults: each one moves a row.
  • Plan the pull-back as your own job. The store does not come back for a gap on the event tested (the recover row), and vCenter and the hosts keep what you need only inside their windows.
  • Land the gap as absence, never as zero: plate 14 is what an extract does with it.

Measure every outage against every hop's window. What outlasts a hop's window is gone from that hop, and only a hop upstream, still inside its own window, can give it back.

The strategy in one breath

Collect once, at the plane that already owns the signal. Decide in region, where the native data lives. Extract by tier: the gold layer daily, the analysis windows hourly and exact, the hot-set native and scoped. Key everything on the pair every system already carries. Beyond vSphere, one collector per domain, raw in the region, one thin tier each, joined through the CMDB. Bind the catalog to a live statkey list, never to a picker. Treat the real-time plane as the truth for a burst and a troubleshooting store for everything else, read it by its rules, in code, and remember that its samples are the ones the store already rolled up. Reconcile absence before it becomes a number.
